Cybersecurity 101: Get Off that Vibe Coded Website

Valentina Falanga & Malayka Mudassar

If you feel like everything online has gotten worse recently, you aren’t imagining things. With the rise of AI, no corner of the internet was left untouched. Of course, this includes app and website development. You may have heard the phrase “vibe coding” being thrown around. Unfortunately, this is an extremely misleading name to anyone unfamiliar with it.

Vibe coding describes the use of almost entirely (or entirely) generative AI to build a website or app. In other words, coding based on “vibe.” Oftentimes, this is someone with very little to no coding experience building an entire app using AI prompts. Without proper oversight, these vibe coded programs are about as well put together as a bridge made of toothpicks and scotch tape. That is to say, they are not exactly stable, or safe.

Apiiro, an application security platform, analyzed code from thousands of code repositories, coding assistants and developers across Fortune 50 enterprises. Their findings showed that AI dropped trivial errors in syntax (spelling errors in the code) by 76%. In turn, they found that architectural design flaws increased by 153%. This includes errors such as broken authorization flows and insecure designs. In simpler terms, it lacks the security measures needed to ward off potential attacks. Quite the tradeoff.

Without a knowledge of cybersecurity, it can be difficult to understand what exactly these security risks mean as a user. It is even more difficult as vibe coding is a fairly new phenomenon, and simply looking up "security risks vibe coding” brings up a few dozen sites aimed towards people looking to fix their vibe coded apps and a few dozen more blogs speaking exclusively in terms your local IT guy probably can’t even understand. So, I’ll do us both a favor and look them up.

IBM describes two types of vibe code induced vulnerabilities. “Slopsquatting” is when attackers use packages that the AI hallucinated. Packages are groups of predefined coding functions that are available for public use. Attackers see the hallucinated package and register it themselves, making it no longer a hallucination. This gives them an in to upload malware directly into the code. A second type of attack is “rules file backdoor,” in which attackers modify the AI’s publicly available rule code. They use characters that are extremely difficult to detect and input malicious instructions into the actual AI. They usually use this to make any code generated by this AI have some kind of vulnerability they can exploit, or a “backdoor” for malware.

More frequently, vibe coded websites tend to have exposed APIs. APIs or application programming interfaces are how apps and websites communicate with the wider internet. When you are signing into something, and choose to sign in with Google - it happens through an API. The weather app accesses the weather bureau’s data through an API. API keys are used to identify and authenticate you when you use an app or website. They are like the ID of your account. Oftentimes, AI does not give API keys the security they need, leading to leaks. An API key leak can lead to unauthorized access to your account and leaves your data exposed for anyone to tamper with. These are only a few of many vulnerabilities AI tends to leave in vibe coded sites that receive no overlook from someone experienced with cybersecurity and programming in general.

Following the temporary shutdown of Archive of Our Own (AO3), many users frantically sought out alternatives. AO3 is a prominent archival site for fanworks, in which users can upload and read fanfiction (fan-written stories expanding on media such as TV shows, video games, etc.) created by the Organization for Transformative Works (OTW). Due to a cybersecurity vulnerability discovered by AI, OTW announced AO3’s extended maintenance, estimating 2-4 weeks. As thousands of fans scrambled for an alternative, some began sharing links to sites meant to act as replacements. Unbeknown to most of these fans, most of these programs were entirely vibe coded- putting their information at serious risk.

While these sites were suspicious at best, one in particular gained rapid attention for its extremely worrying content. Titled “AO3.life,” the site appeared just 3 days after the shutdown and was marketed as an alternative to AO3. It even turned the fanfiction into AI-generated audiobooks, for a price. Slowly, social media posts started appearing as authors discovered that works they had long deleted were available on the site. Others found that works they were still actively working on, and had saved in their drafts, were also available. Concerned, thousands of posts questioning this new site flooded in. Many suspected AO3.life of scraping AO3’s data in order to populate their site. It was also widely suspected that the site was a phishing scam, or an attempt to collect users' data. The site was reported en masse to OTW and requests for the site to be taken down flooded AO3.life. In just a few days, the site was taken down. An apology was issued in which a 17-Year old named Ganesh claimed to have built the site.

While in this instance, the supposed creator of the site claims to not have sinister intentions, this is an example of the way these sites prey on those with little cyberknowledge knowledge. While there is no confirmation from the creator of AO3.life, most believe the site to be vibe coded- as most AO3 alternatives circulating are. This means, even if the creator didn’t intend to misuse the emails inputted by users, the site itself is vulnerable to outside attackers with bad intentions. It is important to be aware of the dangers of these unstable programs, and to never- EVER- put personal information into an untrustworthy site.

It can be hard to tell a vibe coded app or website apart from a trustworthy one, especially to the untrained eye. Preferably, sticking to sites you know are legitimate is the safest. However, if you have to venture out, there are some tells. This site gives examples of common traits of a vibe coded website. Still not confident? Don’t worry, even tech experts can’t tell with 100% accuracy when a site is AI-generated. The best you can do is be aware when visiting new sites and be extra prudent with your personal information.
